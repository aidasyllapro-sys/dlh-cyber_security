# MedDefense Health Systems: The Framework Landscape

**Prepared by:** Aïda Sylla, Security Analyst **Prepared for:** James Chen, Deputy CISO, and Sarah Park, IT Director **Source material:** Direct research against NIST's own CSF 2.0 publication, CIS's own Controls v8 documentation, and ISO/IEC's own 27001:2022 standard summaries, verified independently for this document **Purpose:** Before MedDefense can build a strategy, it needs to understand its tools. This document explains what each of the three major frameworks actually does, where they overlap, and which one (or combination) fits an organization with MedDefense's specific profile.

---

## Part 1: Three-Framework Summary

### NIST Cybersecurity Framework 2.0

The NIST CSF is published by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, a U.S. federal agency, and its current version, CSF 2.0, was released in February 2024. It is designed to give any organization, regardless of size or sector, a common vocabulary and structure for thinking about cybersecurity risk, not a checklist of specific technical controls to implement. It is organized around six Functions (Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, and Recover, with Govern newly added in 2.0 to formally recognize governance as a foundational activity rather than a background assumption), broken down into 22 Categories and 106 Subcategories, plus four maturity Tiers (Partial, Risk Informed, Repeatable, Adaptive) that describe how mature an organization's practices are, and Organizational Profiles that let an organization document its current state and target state. It is typically used by organizations of any size, including small and midsize businesses since the 2.0 revision explicitly expanded its intended audience beyond its original critical-infrastructure focus, as a strategic-level tool to communicate priorities to leadership and the Board, not as a technical implementation guide.

### CIS Controls v8

CIS Controls are published by the Center for Internet Security, a nonprofit organization, with the current version, v8, released in 2021 and refined in v8.1. It is designed to answer a more concrete question than the NIST CSF: given limited time and budget, which specific, actionable defensive actions actually stop the most common real-world attacks first. It is structured around 18 Controls (consolidated down from 20 in the prior version, reorganized around activity rather than who manages a given device) containing 153 total Safeguards, each a single, testable, plain-language action, distributed across three cumulative Implementation Groups: IG1 (56 Safeguards, essential cyber hygiene for organizations with limited security staff and resources), IG2 (adds 74 more Safeguards, for organizations with dedicated IT/security staff and moderate complexity), and IG3 (adds the remaining Safeguards, for organizations facing sophisticated or targeted threats). It is typically used by IT and security teams directly as an implementation checklist, often specifically starting from IG1 as the baseline every organization should meet regardless of sector, and is frequently referenced by cyber insurance providers when evaluating an applicant's actual security practices.

### ISO/IEC 27001

ISO 27001 is published jointly by the International Organization for Standardization and the International Electrotechnical Commission, with the current version being ISO/IEC 27001:2022. Unlike the other two frameworks, it is not primarily a list of technical activities; it defines the requirements for an Information Security Management System (ISMS), a formal, auditable management process for identifying, treating, and continuously improving how an organization manages information security risk. It is structured around 11 core clauses (numbered 0 through 10, with Clauses 4 through 10 containing the mandatory, auditable requirements covering context, leadership, planning, support, operation, performance evaluation, and improvement), supported by Annex A, a reference list of 93 possible security controls grouped into four themes (Organizational, People, Physical, and Technological) from which an organization selects and documents only the ones relevant to its own risk assessment, recorded formally in a Statement of Applicability. It is typically pursued by organizations that need to formally certify their security posture to an external party, customers, regulators, insurers, or business partners, through a third-party audit, and is most common in industries or contracts where a business relationship specifically requires proof of certification, not merely evidence of good practice.

## Part 2: Relationship Map

These three frameworks are not competitors, and treating them as three alternative options to choose between misunderstands what each one is actually for. The useful mental model, and the one this document adopts directly, is that NIST CSF answers "what should we do," providing the strategic categories and vocabulary an organization uses to organize its entire cybersecurity program and communicate about it with a Board or CFO who has no interest in technical minutiae. CIS Controls answers "how should we do it," providing the specific, concrete, testable safeguards that make each of NIST's abstract Functions and Categories real and actionable for the people actually configuring systems day to day. ISO 27001 answers "can we prove we are doing it," providing the formal management-system structure, documentation discipline, and third-party audit process that turns an organization's internal claim of good security into an externally verifiable certification. In practice, a mature security program frequently uses all three together: it adopts NIST CSF as the organizing structure for its overall strategy and Board communication, selects specific CIS Controls Safeguards as the concrete technical actions that satisfy each NIST Category, and, if and when the business need for formal certification arises, builds an ISO 27001 ISMS around the same underlying risk decisions already made through the first two, since CIS explicitly publishes mapping documents connecting its own Safeguards to both NIST CSF and ISO 27001, meaning none of this mapping work needs to be built from scratch.

## Part 3: MedDefense Framework Selection

**Recommendation: NIST CSF 2.0 as the strategic backbone, with CIS Controls v8 Implementation Group 1 as the tactical implementation layer. Full ISO 27001 certification is not recommended at this time.**

This recommendation is built directly around the specific constraints James Chen and Sarah Park described, not a generic best-practice preference. MedDefense has no framework currently in place, meaning the first priority is establishing a common structure the Board, the CFO, and the two-person security team can all use to talk about the same priorities in the same language; NIST CSF 2.0 is built exactly for this purpose; the CIS-published guide to its own controls even describes NIST CSF as offering "strategic structure" while CIS Controls offer "tactical implementation steps," which is the precise division of labor MedDefense needs. With only one Security Analyst and one Deputy CISO, MedDefense cannot realistically staff the kind of large-scale, multi-department implementation effort IG2 or IG3 CIS Controls assume, whereas CIS Controls v8's own IG1 tier was specifically designed for organizations with limited security staff and resources defending lower-sensitivity data, matching MedDefense's actual current staffing directly rather than an aspirational future state. Regarding ISO 27001 specifically: while Sarah Park's concern about auditors asking "which framework do you follow" is legitimate and NIST CSF answers it directly, formal ISO 27001 certification requires a dedicated management-system build-out and a paid third-party certification audit, a resource commitment this two-person team and this project's own $120,000 total budget constraint cannot realistically absorb this year without displacing the technical remediation work already identified as urgent in Project 1x02. MedDefense should adopt ISO 27001's underlying discipline informally, specifically the practice of a documented Statement of Applicability explaining which controls apply and why, since this project's own remediation work already produces exactly that kind of justification, without pursuing the formal certification itself until a specific business driver (a payer contract, a cyber insurance requirement, or a Board decision to invest further) makes the cost justified. This combination gives MedDefense a defensible answer to the auditor's question today, a concrete implementation checklist its two-person team can actually execute, and a clear, lower-cost path toward formal certification later if and when it becomes a genuine business requirement rather than a symbolic one.
