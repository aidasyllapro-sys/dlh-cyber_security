# MedDefense Health Systems: The Governance Architecture

**Prepared by:** Aïda Sylla, Security Analyst **Prepared for:** James Chen, Deputy CISO **Purpose:** A strategy without governance is a document that sits on a shelf. This document defines exactly who decides, who executes, who is consulted, and who is informed for every major security activity at MedDefense, and resolves the specific authority disputes James Chen raised directly (endpoint security ownership between IT and Security, and departmental data autonomy).

**Notes on the judgment calls behind this matrix, kept deliberately restrained: this table describes who prepares, who executes, who is asked, and who decides, and it avoids implying that any preparatory or supporting role carries decision authority it does not have.**

**Security policy approval and Risk acceptance decisions** hold the CEO as the sole Accountable party, full stop; nothing in this matrix should be read as the CEO's approval being a formality once James has finished his analysis. James is Responsible for preparing the policy draft or the risk analysis and bringing a recommendation forward, the same evidentiary work this program's three prior projects produced, but preparing a recommendation is not the same as owning the decision, and this matrix keeps that line clear rather than blurred. Sarah and the Department Heads are Consulted, not Informed only, because their operational and clinical input materially shapes what James ultimately recommends and what the CEO ultimately approves, particularly for any provision touching patient care systems or departmental data handling, which is the direct governance answer to Dr. Patel's stated position: his input as a Department Head is substantively sought and weighed, not a courtesy, even though the final decision authority sits with the CEO alone, not with him and not with James.

**Security budget approval** follows the identical logic: James is Responsible for building and presenting the business case, exactly the analytical work this program has produced, while the CEO holds sole Accountability as the one who ultimately authorizes the spend. The Security Analyst is Consulted here, not Responsible, since producing the underlying quantitative analysis is support work that feeds James's business case rather than a decision-execution role of its own.

**Vulnerability remediation and Incident response execution** shift the Accountable party to James rather than the CEO, a deliberate distinction from the governance rows above: these are operational security activities, not organization-wide business decisions, and James holds day-to-day accountability for the program's execution once the CEO has already approved its existence and budget. Sarah's team is Responsible for the physical work of applying patches and configuration changes, and the Security Analyst is Consulted rather than Responsible here as well, a deliberate correction from an earlier draft of this matrix that risked making technical and security staff appear to hold execution ownership beyond their actual role: the Analyst's real contribution, triage, validation, and tracking, is real and necessary, but it supports Sarah's execution rather than standing as an independent, accountable line of work. Department Heads remain Consulted rather than Informed only, since 1x02's own remediation work (Task 19) established that changes touching medical devices require Clinical or Biomedical Engineering sign-off before IT can act at all, a genuine operational dependency, not a courtesy notification.

**Security awareness training** is the one row where Department Heads are marked Responsible rather than Consulted, and this is worth distinguishing directly from the governance rows above: this is not a decision-ownership assignment, it reflects a specific, already-evidenced operational fact. 1x01's Human Vector analysis found training completion varying as low as 58% at one site, meaning a centrally-mandated program with no local departmental enforcement has already been proven insufficient at MedDefense; Department Heads hold real execution responsibility here (ensuring their own staff complete training) in addition to Sarah's platform-level responsibility, not a governance or approval role.

## Part 2: Role Definitions

**Data Owner.** The Data Owner is the individual with clinical or business authority over a specific data domain, accountable for deciding who should have access to that data and for what legitimate purpose, but critically, this authority operates within organizational policy, not above it. At MedDefense, Department Heads (Dr. Patel for Cardiology's patient data, and the equivalent role holder in each other clinical department) are the Data Owners for the data their department generates and uses clinically. This directly resolves the tension James described: Dr. Patel is correct that he holds real authority as Data Owner over how his department's patient data is used clinically, but Data Owner is not the same role as Data Controller, and his ownership does not grant him unilateral authority to override organization-wide security policy or access controls, exactly the confusion driving his stated position.

**Data Controller.** Under standard data governance terminology, the Data Controller is the entity that determines the purposes and means of processing data, the party ultimately legally accountable for how data is handled across the organization as a whole. At MedDefense, this is the organization itself, MedDefense Health Systems, represented by the CEO as its accountable executive. This is the specific role that limits Dr. Patel's claimed autonomy: the Data Controller, not any individual Data Owner, sets the binding, organization-wide policy framework (access controls, retention rules, third-party sharing restrictions) that every Data Owner must operate within, regardless of clinical seniority or departmental authority.

**Data Processor.** The Data Processor is a third party that handles data on the Controller's behalf and under the Controller's instructions, without independent authority over how that data is used. At MedDefense, this role belongs to the organization's technology vendors with direct access to patient data, most directly MedTech Solutions, whose EHR maintenance access was assessed directly in 1x01's Supply Chain Question (Task 5), and to a lesser degree Microsoft (O365) and Sophos. These vendors process MedDefense's data under contract and instruction; they do not own it or independently decide how it may be used.

**Data Custodian/Steward.** The Data Custodian holds the operational and technical responsibility for actually implementing the protections the Owner and Controller require, managing the servers, access controls, backups, and technical infrastructure that store and secure the data day to day, without independent authority to decide who should have access or why. At MedDefense, Sarah Park, as IT Director, holds this role for the underlying technical infrastructure, and this is the precise distinction that resolves the second tension James described directly: Sarah does not own security policy simply because IT manages the endpoints; she is the Custodian responsible for correctly implementing the security requirements that Data Owners and the Data Controller establish, a distinct and narrower role than ownership.

## Part 3: The CISO Question

**Consequences of the vacant CISO position.** MedDefense currently has no single, fully-empowered executive with formal, undisputed authority over the entire security program, and this gap is directly visible in the governance dispute James described: without a CISO whose positional authority is unambiguous, Sarah, James, and Department Heads each have a partially reasonable claim to ownership over overlapping security responsibilities, with no clear tiebreaker beyond whoever advocates most forcefully in the moment, exactly the "whoever shouts loudest" problem James named. It also means the strategic, Board-facing work this entire three-project program has produced, quantitative risk framing, executive communication, and budget justification, has been performed by a Deputy without the formal title or standing that typically carries the most weight in a Board or CFO conversation, a real credibility gap when Robert Kim challenges the numbers. Finally, the absence of a named, accountable security executive is itself a governance gap that regulators and auditors increasingly expect to see filled, directly consistent with Sarah's own admission to auditors that MedDefense follows "no framework, formally," a similar visibility problem now extending to organizational structure, not just technical practice.

**Recommendation: a virtual/fractional CISO (vCISO), not a full-time hire, at this stage.** This recommendation is built directly around the budget constraint established throughout this program: MedDefense's entire annual security budget is $120,000, and 1x02's own remediation planning (Task 20) already showed that figure strained to its limit by technical remediation costs alone, with only $35,100 in uncommitted reserve remaining before this governance question is even considered. A full-time CISO's compensation, even for a modest regional healthcare organization, would typically consume an amount comparable to or exceeding MedDefense's entire current security budget on salary alone, which is not a realistic option this year without displacing the technical remediation work this program has already justified as urgent. A vCISO arrangement provides the specific thing this gap requires, executive-level authority, Board credibility, and governance tie-breaking capability, at a materially lower cost, typically structured as a part-time monthly retainer rather than a full salary, while James continues to serve as the day-to-day Deputy CISO and the Security Analyst continues the technical and analytical work this program has already demonstrated. This is recommended as a near-term bridge, not a permanent ceiling: as MedDefense's security program matures and its budget grows in future years, converting to a full-time CISO should be revisited directly, once the organization's risk profile and program scope can justify the fuller investment.
