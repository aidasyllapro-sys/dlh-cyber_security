# MedDefense Health Systems: Asset Criticality Assessment

**Prepared by:** Aïda Sylla, Security Analyst **Prepared for:** James Chen, Deputy CISO (for Board presentation) **Source material:** Asset Registry (`7-asset_registry.md`) and all prior project findings (Tasks 0–7) **Purpose:** Evaluate the criticality of MedDefense's asset categories using CIA-based analysis, calibrated to healthcare-specific consequences, to support Board-level prioritization decisions.

**Scale used:**

|Level|Definition|
|---|---|
|Critical|Compromise directly threatens patient safety, causes regulatory violation, or halts clinical operations|
|High|Compromise causes significant operational disruption, financial loss, or data exposure|
|Medium|Compromise causes moderate disruption, recoverable within standard procedures|
|Low|Compromise has minimal operational or security impact|

**Note on figures used below:** All operational figures cited (bed count, staff counts, study volumes, contract values, incident durations) are drawn directly from documentation and events already established elsewhere in this project (Tasks 0–7). No patient counts, financial-loss estimates, or breach-cost figures are invented. MedDefense's documentation does not contain a specific patient-record count or a quantified breach-cost estimate, so none is asserted here. Where a number would need to be estimated rather than cited from a source, this is stated explicitly rather than presented as fact.

## Top 5 Most Critical Assets

**1. EHR System (ehr-srv-01 / ehr-db-01)** This is the single asset James Chen's own framing points to directly: if it goes down, physicians are prescribing blind. It is the authoritative clinical record for every patient at Central and Westside, it has already caused a real, 9-hour clinical disruption (Incident E) and a real, 6-hour data-integrity incident affecting all three sites (Incident C), and its database (ehr-db-01) is reachable from the entire internal network rather than being restricted to the application server it serves. No other single asset combines this breadth of dependency with this direct a link to clinical decision-making.

**2. ad-dc-01 (Primary Domain Controller)** Nearly every other system in the registry (the EHR, billing, file shares, and the majority of Windows endpoints) depends on this asset for authentication. A compromise here does not fail one application; it can simultaneously undermine trust in every downstream login across the organization. This risk is made worse, not better, by the fact that its intended redundancy (ad-dc-02) is explicitly excluded from the nightly backup job, meaning the organization's identity backbone has less protected redundancy than its own architecture implies.

**3. BD Alaris Infusion Pumps (~120 units)** Of every asset in this registry, this is the one where a security compromise translates most directly and immediately into physical harm to a patient. A falsified or manipulated dosage setting is not a data problem, it is a patient-safety emergency. This is not a hypothetical: the pumps are on the same flat network as general workstations, Marcus explicitly flagged them as reachable by anyone on the network, and the manufacturer has already issued a security bulletin (18 months old, unactioned) recommending the network isolation these pumps still do not have.

**4. MRI Scanner & Control Workstation (WS-RAD-01)** A $2.1 million capital asset, 6 years into a 12-year expected operational life, running an operating system that has not received a security patch in over a decade, and cannot, without voiding its medical device certification. It supports approximately 45 studies per day and sits on the same unsegmented network as every general-purpose workstation in the hospital. It is critical both for what it is worth operationally and clinically, and for how structurally difficult it is to secure through conventional means (see the dedicated compensating-control strategy in Task 6).

**5. Backup & Recovery Infrastructure (NAS-01 / backup-srv-01)** This asset earns its place in the top five not because of what it does day-to-day, but because of what MedDefense has no alternative to if it fails. It is the only documented recovery path for six of the organization's most important systems, including the EHR and the domain controller, yet it sits in the same room, rack row, and network as the systems it is meant to protect, has never been the subject of a full disaster-recovery test, and its one partial recovery test took 6 hours for a single server. If any of the top 4 assets above is compromised at the same time as this one, a realistic scenario given billing-srv-01's documented ransomware history, MedDefense currently has no verified way back to normal operations.
